Evaluation of the ONH Expert Connector Search
Overview
As part of a benchmark user test for the Oslo Nye Høyskole (ONH) Expert Connector , I evaluated the
retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG) search engine that links user queries to staff expertise. The goal was
to  determine  whether  the  application  correctly  identifies  the  most  relevant  experts,  ranks  them
appropriately and provides attributable evidence. To conduct the evaluation I first built a  gold‑standard
staff‑expertise reference, drawing on the official Finn en ekspert page and staff profile pages on the ONH
website as well as available publications on the NVA database. This reference lists dozens of topics (e.g.
Arbeidsmiljø,  Anatomi,  Digitalisering,  Afrikas Horn) and maps them to staff members who have declared
expertise in those areas. I then crafted a diverse set of 64 search queries covering psychology/HR,
economics  and  administration,  health/medicine,  international  relations  and  cross‑cutting  themes.  Each
query was executed in the ONH Expert Connector , and I recorded the actual results alongside the expected
experts,  missing  experts,  unexpected  results  and  evidence  quality  in  a  structured  log  (see
onh_test_log.md).
Staff‑Expertise Reference (Gold Standard)
The Finn en ekspert page groups ONH expertise into categories such as Psykologi og HR, Økonomi og
administrasjon,  Helse, medisin og ernæring and  Statsvitenskap og internasjonale relasjoner. Each
topic on that page points to one or more staff profiles, providing a reliable mapping between expertise and
individuals.  For  example,  under  psychology/HR,  topics  like  Arbeidsmiljø and  Arbeidspsykologi map  to
Anniken Grønstad, Ingvild Sagberg, Linda Mari Seem Sundvik and Mona Berthelsen;  Kritisk psykologi
maps  to  Erik   Nakkerud,  Sandra   Gabrielsen  and  Joshua   Phelps;  and  Motivasjon and  AI   i   utdanning
og   vurderingsformer map  to  Eirik   Kjelstrup.  In  the  economics  category,  Digitalisering is  assigned  to
Kateryna Reiby and  Endringsledelse to Johan Bergh. In health/medicine, topics such as  Anatomi and
Cellebiologi list  Stian   Walle   Larsen,  Suvi   Sagbraaten,  Elin   Lund   Transeth  and  Ulrike   Liisberg,  while
Diabetes and  Epidemiologi map to Dagfinn Aune. For international relations,  Afrikas Horn and  Autoritære
regimer map to Kjetil Tronvoll, Spionasje to Stig Stenslie and Amerikansk politikk to Hilde Eliassen Restad.
This reference enabled me to identify which staff should appear for each query and provided justification
for expected rankings. Where additional context was needed, I consulted staff profile pages to confirm
areas of expertise (e.g. Eirik Kjelstrup’s profile highlights his work on decision‑making, emotions and stress
).

Summary of Search Performance
Strengths
Correct retrieval for some well‑defined topics. When queries exactly matched the topic names on
the official list, the system often returned the correct expert(s) with appropriate ranking. Examples
include:
arbeidspsykologi → Mona Berthelsen, Linda Mari Seem Sundvik and Brita Bjørkelo appear , matching
the official list with correct ordering (severity Low). 
digitalisering and anatomy/cell biology/biochemistry queries returned Kateryna Reiby,
Suvi Sagbraaten/Stian Walle Larsen or Øyvind Jacobsen with high precision.
Several marketing queries (digital markedsføring, finans, regnskap, merkevareledelse, 
kampanjeplanlegging) produced the expected lecturers in top positions.
In international relations, topics like Afrikas Horn and Spionasje correctly surfaced Kjetil Tronvoll and
Stig Stenslie respectively, demonstrating the model can retrieve some niche geopolitical expertise.
Support for Norwegian queries and compound nouns. Many queries used Norwegian compound
phrases  (e.g.  beslutningspsykologi,  livsstilssykdommer).  The  system  generally  tokenised  these
correctly, matching them to staff keywords and returning relevant experts (e.g. Eirik Kjelstrup for
beslutningspsykologi, Ella Marie Heyerdahl for livsstilssykdommer).
Key Failure Modes
Despite some successes, numerous systematic issues emerged:
High false‑negative rate for many topics. In 25 out of 64 queries the primary expected expert was
missing entirely, including alkoholmisbruk ungdom (missing Anne Kjeldsen), kritisk psykologi (missing
Erik   Nakkerud  and  co.),  beslutningsbias,  stressmestring,  graviditetstrening,  radikalisering,  FN and
autoritaere regimer. These failures suggest that the retrieval stage is not indexing or matching the
official keywords effectively.
Frequent false positives and category leakage. Many queries returned staff from unrelated fields.
For instance, arbeidsmiljø og hybridarbeid produced a long list of names from international relations
and nutrition; spionasje and fred og konflikt included political scientists not connected to espionage;
and  diabetes returned a psychologist (Line Wisting) before the epidemiologist Dagfinn Aune. The
presence  of  Beate   Mangset  (a  health  ethicist)  in  numerous  unrelated  queries  (e.g.
kommunikasjonstrening,  arbeidskonflikter,  styrketrening)  indicates  the  ranking  model  is  overly
influenced by generic terms like “veiledning” found in many profiles.
Poor ranking within retrieved candidates. Even when the expected expert was retrieved, they
were  often  not  ranked  first.  E.g.  AI   i   utdanning placed  Eirik   Kjelstrup  fourth  behind  two
digital‑marketing  lecturers;  endringsledelse placed  the  actual  course  leader  Johan   Bergh  below
Sandra   Gabrielsen;  komparativ  politikk put  Henry   Allen  ahead  of  the  comparativist
Jonas Bergan Dræge. The algorithm seems to weight generic similarity or recency over domain
relevance.

Evidence/citation mismatch. The RAG system surfaces evidence snippets next to each expert, but
these are sometimes generic (e.g. listing courses taught) rather than directly supporting the query.
For example, the evidence for Eirik Kjelstrup in AI i utdanning referenced his psychological research
rather than his AI work; some results displayed tags not obviously related to the query. In several
cases (e.g. FN, bistand) the sole result had no connection to the query at all.
Limited handling of synonyms and language variants. Queries using synonyms, abbreviations or
adjacent  phrases  often  failed.  Kommunikasjonstrening (communication  training)  and  teambygging
(team building) did not retrieve the organisational psychology lecturers, while  autoritaere regimer
returned  a  comparativist  instead  of  the  authoritarian‑regime  specialists  Kjetil   Tronvoll  and
Stig Stenslie. Abbreviations such as FN (United Nations) matched “Folkehelse ernæring” rather than
the intended international‑relations term.
Patterns of Errors
Keyword leakage across categories: The search tends to match keywords at surface level, ignoring
disciplinary context. Staff who mention “stress” or “veiledning” in any context were retrieved for
queries about stress management or team building, irrespective of their field. Similarly, nutrition
lecturers appeared for UN‑related queries because their tags contained two letters matching the
acronym.
Insufficient use of official topic mapping: The RAG pipeline does not appear to prioritise the
authoritative mapping between topics and experts provided on the ONH website. Incorporating this
structured knowledge as a high‑weight signal could dramatically improve recall and ranking.
Lack of query expansion: Many queries could benefit from synonym expansion (e.g. beslutningsbias
→ decision bias) and morphological normalization to capture variations (e.g. arbeidsmiljø vs 
arbeidsmiljø og hybridarbeid). The current system seems brittle to such variation.
Minimal filtering of evidence snippets: The sources shown often consist of full profile descriptions.
Shorter , targeted excerpts that mention the query terms would improve user trust and highlight
relevance. In some cases, the evidence for irrelevant experts did not contain the query at all.
Recommendations
Incorporate  the  official  “Finn   en   ekspert”  mapping  into  the  retrieval  pipeline. Use  the
structured  list  of  topics  and  associated  staff  as  a  knowledge  base.  When  a  query  matches  or
semantically relates to a listed topic, those experts should be given a very high prior probability. This
would prevent obvious misses such as alkoholmisbruk ungdom and kritisk psykologi.
Improve semantic search with synonym and abbreviation handling. Implement query expansion
to include synonyms, morphological variants and common abbreviations. For example, map  FN to
“United   Nations”,  autoritaere  regimer to  “authoritarian  regimes”,  and  recognise  that
kommunikasjonstrening relates to organisational communication training. This could be achieved by
enriching the embedding model with a Norwegian thesaurus or manually curated synonym lists.
Apply  category‑aware  filtering  and  weighting. Each  staff  member  belongs  to  one  or  more
categories (psychology/HR, economics, health, international relations). When a query clearly falls
within a category, experts from unrelated categories should be heavily down‑weighted. This would
reduce false positives such as nutrition lecturers appearing in political science queries.

Re‑train  ranking  with  relevance  feedback. Use  the  gold‑standard  dataset  constructed  here
(queries  with  expected  experts)  to  train  or  fine‑tune  the  ranking  model.  The  objective  should
prioritise  recall  of  true  experts  and  penalise  the  elevation  of  irrelevant  candidates.
Human‑in‑the‑loop evaluations could iteratively refine weights.
Improve evidence snippet extraction. The RAG system should return concise passages from staff
profiles or publications that explicitly mention the query topic. This may involve chunking profiles
into smaller segments and scoring them against the query. Irrelevant evidence should be filtered
out.
Support multi‑language queries. Although Norwegian queries were accepted, some English or
mixed‑language  queries  (e.g.  “cognitive  remediation  therapy”)  performed  poorly.  Adding
multilingual  embedding  models  and  translation  layers  could  expand  coverage  to  English
terminology used by students and faculty.
Suggested Benchmark Structure for Regression Testing
Dataset: Use the 64 queries and their expected experts provided in onh_test_log.md as the
baseline benchmark. For each query, include the list of expected experts and a severity rating
reflecting the impact of missing them.
Metrics: Measure recall (percentage of expected experts retrieved), precision (percentage of
retrieved experts that are relevant) and ranking quality (e.g. mean reciprocal rank of the top
expected expert). Also assess evidence quality by checking that returned snippets mention the query
terms.
Regression procedure: After each update to the search algorithm, re‑run the benchmark queries
and record metrics. Highlight regressions where previously correct queries now fail or ranking
deteriorates. Use the severity ratings to prioritise fixes.
Conclusion
The ONH Expert Connector shows promise but currently struggles to consistently retrieve and rank the
most relevant experts. Reliance on loose keyword matching leads to high false‑negative and false‑positive
rates, and the ranking often misplaces key experts beneath unrelated staff. By integrating the authoritative
expertise  mapping  from  the  ONH  website,  enhancing  semantic  understanding  and  implementing
category‑aware filtering and better evidence extraction, the system can significantly improve both precision
and  user  trust.  The  comprehensive  benchmark  developed  here  provides  a  foundation  for  iterative
improvements and future regression testing.
